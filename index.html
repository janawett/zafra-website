Our Story

Centuries ago, Colima's salt traveled over 300 miles to reach the Aztec emperor — a tribute so prized that kingdoms fought to possess it.

That lagoon still exists. The salt still comes from there.

Laguna de Cuyutlán, on Mexico's Pacific coast, sits on a subsoil of mineral-rich earth and volcanic sand. Through traditional wells, the salineros draw brine to the surface — never touching the open ocean. The brine is laid across clay-lined beds, dried by the sun and wind of the Pacific, and harvested by hand.

Unrefined. Nothing added. Nothing removed.

Every year, when the rains dry and the lagoon settles, the zafra begins.

Still made by hand.
Filtered by the Earth Itself

The water that becomes Zafra doesn't come from the open ocean.

Through traditional wells, the salineros draw brine to the surface from a subsoil of mineral-rich earth and volcanic sand — nature's own filter. The brine is then laid into shallow clay-lined beds where the Pacific sun and coastal wind slowly evaporate it, until only salt remains.
