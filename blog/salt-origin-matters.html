Why Where Your Salt Comes From Actually Matters

Salt is one of the oldest traded commodities on earth. For most of human history, its origin was everything — empires were built on it, wars were fought over it, and the word "salary" traces back to Roman soldiers paid in salt. Somewhere along the way, that changed. Today most sea salt is sold with vague origin claims, a beautiful coastline photo, and the implicit suggestion that all salt is basically the same.

It isn't.

Where salt comes from — the specific body of water, the geology beneath it, the climate above it, the method used to harvest it — shapes what ends up in the bag. Here's why that matters.

Not all ocean water is the same

The ocean isn't a uniform body of water. Its mineral composition varies by region, depth, and proximity to land. Coastal lagoons and inland saltwater bodies develop their own distinct mineral profiles, shaped by the geology of the surrounding land, the flow of freshwater into the basin, and the sediment beneath.

Laguna de Cuyutlán, on Mexico's Pacific coast in the state of Colima, is a case in point. The lagoon sits in a region with volcanic subsoil — a geological feature that influences the mineral content of water filtering through it over centuries. The brine drawn from traditional wells at this site reflects that local geology in its mineral composition, in a way that open-ocean evaporated salt simply cannot replicate.

This is the same principle behind why wine from different vineyards tastes different even when made from the same grape variety. Terroir — the concept that the land itself leaves a mark on what grows from it — applies to salt as much as it does to grapes.

How salt is extracted changes what you get

There are two dominant methods for producing sea salt: open pan evaporation from surface seawater, and brine extraction from underground or lagoon wells.

Surface evaporation is the most common commercial method. Seawater is pumped into large shallow pans and left to evaporate under the sun, leaving salt crystals behind. It's efficient and scalable, but the salt it produces reflects the composition of surface seawater — including whatever is suspended in it.

Brine extraction from wells is older and less common. At Laguna de Cuyutlán, saltworkers draw brine from underground wells sunk into mineral-rich subsoil. The water that feeds these wells has been slowly filtered through layers of volcanic rock and sediment — a natural purification process that has been occurring for centuries. The resulting brine has a different mineral character than surface seawater, and produces salt with a distinct flavor profile.

The Pacific sun and coastal wind do the rest, evaporating the brine slowly and naturally until the salt crystallizes. No heat accelerants, no chemical additives, no anti-caking agents.

Hand harvesting versus mechanical processing

After crystallization, what happens to the salt matters too.

Most commercial sea salt is mechanically harvested — scraped up by machines, conveyed through industrial equipment, and processed at scale. The efficiency is real. So is the loss of nuance: mechanical processing tends to produce uniform, fine crystals and can introduce contact with metal surfaces throughout the line.

At Laguna de Cuyutlán, the Sociedad Cooperativa de Salineros de Colima — a salt cooperative established in 1925 — harvests by hand. Workers wade into the shallow crystallization beds and gather salt with shovels and rakes, the same way it has been done for generations. The result is coarse, irregular crystals that retain the full mineral profile of the brine without mechanical interference.

Hand harvesting is slower and more labor-intensive than industrial methods. It's also the reason the texture, mineral content, and flavor of the salt remain intact from lagoon to bag.

Why named origin matters more than regional claims

"Pacific sea salt" is a label that tells you almost nothing. The Pacific Ocean covers 165 million square kilometers. Salt harvested from an industrial operation in one part of the Pacific is not the same as salt extracted from a century-old cooperative lagoon in another.

A named origin — a specific lagoon, a named cooperative, a verifiable harvest site — is traceable. It means a supply chain with accountability at every step, rather than salt aggregated from multiple sources and sold under a generic geographic claim.

Traceability also matters for testing. When a salt has a named, fixed source, testing results from one batch are meaningful for future batches. When origin is vague or aggregated, a clean test result from one shipment tells you little about the next.

What to look for on the label

When evaluating sea salt by origin, these are the signals worth checking:

  1. A named harvest location, not just a country or ocean. "Colima, Mexico" is better than "Mexico." "Laguna de Cuyutlán" is better still — it's a specific, verifiable place.
  2. A named producer or cooperative. Who actually harvests it? A named producer means accountability. A generic supply chain means you're trusting the brand's sourcing decisions without any way to verify them.
  3. Extraction method disclosed. Surface evaporation and brine well extraction produce meaningfully different results. If the brand doesn't say, it's almost certainly the former.
  4. Harvest method disclosed. Hand-harvested versus mechanically processed affects crystal structure, mineral retention, and texture. It's also a proxy for scale — large industrial operations are almost always mechanical.
